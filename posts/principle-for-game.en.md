# **Preface**

The author's purpose in writing this article is to discuss why "a game" is fun. There are many games in the world, yet people always have their own preferences. The author has seldom seen game analysis grounded in reason and logic that probes a game's gameplay and core design ideas; reviewers almost always set out from an emotional angle. Hence this article — an essay on what a game's gameplay really is, and why it is really fun.

Many people may challenge the author, arguing that since the author has never designed a game himself, his words can only be regarded as armchair speculation. I do not intend to offer any rebuttal to this; this article is written only for people who are both tolerant and open-minded, and rich in insight and discernment.

This article is divided into five parts:

First, this article will introduce the definition of "resources." In the second part, combining several real examples, this article will describe in detail the gameplay born of "resources" — the logic chain. In the third part, this article will probe deeper into the "length" and "harmony" of logic chains. In the fourth part, this article will attempt to analyze several games using the "resource–logic chain" theory. Finally, in the fifth part, this article will point out some of this theory's shortcomings.

# **Resources**

## **The Definition of Resources**

Resources are used extensively in all kinds of games. For example, the four basic resources in *Kantai Collection*, modeled after old-school RTS games: fuel, ammunition, steel, and bauxite. Or the food and fresh water you must secure in some survival games; and the gold that must be earned by last-hitting creeps in *Dota 2* and *League of Legends* — all of these are resources. But there are too many kinds, far too tangled and miscellaneous, so the author generalized them, arriving at the definition of a resource:

                                                                        Everything that can be obtained from within a game and put to use by the player is a resource.

"Obtainable from within the game" defines a resource's origin: only what can be acquired in-game counts as a resource. Hence the objection some raise — "spending my time doing something is also spending resources to play the game" — does not hold. In addition, things that exist in the game yet cannot be obtained are not resources (for example, in *League of Legends* players cannot demolish terrain to acquire terrain buildings). At the same time, this sentence brings up a question of its own: can things like "diamonds" or "crystals" obtained through monetary payment count as resources? This article will discuss that question later.

And "usable by the player" defines a resource's function. Acquired resources usually serve many purposes, such as strengthening oneself, producing goods, reshaping the world, and so on. This article will study the functions of resources in more detail in the discussion of logic chains.

## **Responding to Some Criticism**

Obviously, this definition of resources appears overly broad, even somewhat baffling. The author will explain all of this in the classification methods for resources that follow.

In addition, the author admits that games with no resource element whatsoever do exist, such as *Monument Valley*. Its gameplay has nothing to do with resources (a corresponding explanation exists, but it is a bit of a stretch, and will be offered later). But such cases are few and far between after all, so this theory still applies in most places.

## **How to Classify Resources**

Although classifying resources is of little significance, for the sake of logical rigor this article will discuss several methods of classifying resources. These methods will come into play later when real cases are analyzed with the "resource–logic chain theory." If you consider yourself strong of comprehension, you may also skip this subsection. Finally, please note that the classification of resources is actually quite fuzzy most of the time; there is no need to deliberately distinguish them.

## **Renewable Resources and One-Time Resources**

By whether they can be renewed, resources can be classified into renewable resources and one-time resources. Renewable resources can be drawn from the game world in an unending stream (for example, the gold mines of *Warcraft*, or the mineral patches and vespene geysers of *StarCraft*; here the relatively infinite also counts as renewable). Renewable resources are usually a game's cornerstone: as elements that can be obtained without limit, they hold up the entire high-rise edifice of the game.

One-time resources can usually be obtained only once. They may be the reward for defeating a powerful boss, or the final reward of an event (for example, in *Terraria*, defeating a boss yields a consumable that one-time expands the accessory slot; many mobile games grant a limited character after clearing an event). One-time resources are usually powerful in effect, granting the player a useful upgrade or carrying great commemorative significance (though one seldom sees anyone showing these off, gazing at a bag full of commemorative badges does put one in a good mood).

## **Instant-Effect Resources and Delayed-Effect Resources**

Some resources take effect the instant they are obtained; these may be called instant-effect resources. Some resources require a measure of processing after being obtained, or a stretch of waiting, before they take effect; these may be called delayed-effect resources.

Most resources take effect immediately. From a certain angle, this conforms to the now widely accepted theory of games: what makes games fun is instant feedback — an action receives its reward at once. There are many examples of this, which will not be belabored here.

But adding a modicum of delayed-effect resources has its benefits too. Take cooking a meal as an example. When you smell the aroma of the food and fantasize about how delicious it will be fresh out of the pot, your anticipation for the meal rises. This is probably why mobile games always add an entertaining gacha animation: while you watch these animations, your anticipation for what you are about to receive is driven very high. By the same token, the cutscene before a boss fight also greatly heightens the fun of the fight itself. Regrettably, this pleasure always takes effect only once; the second time around, players will skip it (unless you keep changing it). An example worth referencing is the pre-battle cutscene of Genichiro in *Sekiro* — most model mods use that cutscene as their test footage.

Another significance of delayed-effect resources is investment and storage. For example, stocks or futures that run ten cycles; devices that extend the storage life of resources (such as drying racks in survival games). Processed resources always work better — which also accords with people's conventional understanding and fond hopes: the older the wine, the mellower its fragrance.

## **Permanent Resources and Temporary Resources**

Some resources remain effective forever once obtained, for example upgraded character equipment (it may need repairs, but a character's equipment is part of the character), adding a card to your deck, and so on. Permanent resources accompany the player through a whole match (sometimes including the next one).

Some resources bring only a temporary buff, typically taking the form of various potions and blessing spells across all kinds of games. And some temporary buffs even exact a heavy price, for example the End of the Cycle in *Stellaris*. The common explanation for such a price is equivalent exchange — offering up certain things in exchange for great power (usually sanity and life, as determined on the basis of general emotional intuition).

Please note: be as cautious as possible with temporary buffs. For they affect game difficulty — if difficulty is calibrated against the unbuffed state, players will find the game too easy; if calibrated against buffed strength, players will find it too hard. If a thing can possibly be abused, it will be abused, and it may even force people to abuse it. How to grasp the right measure therein is not within the scope of this article; it will be discussed another time, if the chance arises.

## **Survival Resources and Development Resources**

Some resources serve to sustain survival; lacking them means game over. For example, health, hunger, body temperature, and so on — designs commonly seen in survival games. Many RPGs also feature a health design. Whatever sustains survival is a survival resource.

Some resources, though of no benefit to survival, are things without which you cannot step into the next stage of the game — for example, materials for climbing the tech tree, or keys that unlock levels. Such resources clearly partition the game into distinct stages, and may be called development resources. To quote a famous saying here: "Though it cannot be eaten, it glitters."

## **Other Classification Methods**

There are many more classification methods for resources, such as finite versus infinite resources, allocating resources versus acquiring resources... The world is vast, and one cannot list every method of classifying resources in one go. The several listed above were chosen not because they are especially important, but because they will be put to use in the later chapters of this article.

# **From Resources to Logic**

This section is the true core of this article. Here, the author will argue what a game's gameplay actually is.

## **Resources Drive Action**

In this article's theory, resources are held to be the fundamental driving force of player action. Players act because they want to obtain resources — it is not that the player's actions bring forth resources. This point has suffered some criticism, and so the author will argue the resource-driven theory in more detail.

Let us turn our gaze to a more distant past. The games of that time were usually crafted with great ingenuity: they had no tutorials, nor today's fashionable "weak-player protection" (incidentally, this was first proposed by Yuzhong, intended to teach players of somewhat weaker comprehension how to play complex games — though understood from another angle, it means their games did not supply enough effective logic). Yet players could always get the gameplay with effortless ease, and happily spend several hours playing. By this theory's explanation, this is because those games presented simple "logic": with an empty stomach, the player will naturally figure out a way to find something edible to eat; with two blocks of the same color and shape before one's eyes, the player will figure out a way to put them together. Such logic is self-evident and requires no instruction.

So, from these cases one may conclude: if you place a resource before the player's eyes, the player will always rack their brains to do something with it. This is human curiosity and human instinct, and it is also the true essence of work — using the resources around you to solve problems, create products, and obtain happiness.

So the resource–logic theory holds that resources drive players to act; it is the player's desire to obtain resources that drives the player to make the efforts and attempts at obtaining them, and this process is precisely the process of playing the game.

## **The Different Stages of Player Behavior**

From the above, player actions are in fact governed by the game's resources. If your game has a gun and bullets, the player is certain to shoot them. Here, the author divides player behavior into several stages:

In the first stage, players try to familiarize themselves with the game, understand its gameplay, and savor the pleasure of exploration. In the second stage, players already have a certain understanding of the game and know how to obtain the resources they need according to their own wants. In the third stage, players fully understand the game and wish to seek some challenge and breakthrough within it.

First-stage players are merely exploring the gameplay (newbie onboarding is another question, outside this article's scope — or at least outside this section's scope), while third-stage players transcend the gameplay (for example, challenging every boss without taking damage, clearing the game under such-and-such conditions, and so on). So the behavior of those two kinds will not be discussed here; instead we discuss only the behavior of second-stage players, for they are the ones enjoying the gameplay, and they are our principal object of study.

## **Awei's Survival Game**

Now suppose a player is in the second stage; we shall call him Awei. Overly abstract exposition only hinders understanding, so we will discuss the situation under some concrete cases.

Consider now the survival game Awei is playing. Awei's character is very hungry and must find some **food (obviously a survival resource, a renewable resource, and an instant-effect resource; the resources involved below will not be spelled out again, and will be marked in bold)**, so he decides to go into the forest and pick up a **stone** and **a long wooden stick** to bring back, craft them into a **stone spear**, and set out to hunt a **wild boar**. In a battle resolved by opposed dice rolls, Awei — relying on the bonus of the **stone spear** as well as **his own skills** — successfully defeats the **wild boar**. Awei obtains plenty of **meat**; after cooking it in a **pot** together with **carrots** and **some seasonings**, Awei obtains **a big pot of delicious meat stew**. After eating a hearty meal, he finds that a great deal of **meat** remains unfinished, so he cures it with **salt**, drying it for preservation, and produces **cured meat**.

In Awei's example, we clearly see how resources drive the player through the game. When the player's stomach growls, he is bound to take action to get something to eat. And the process of obtaining food is exactly the process of Awei playing and experiencing the gameplay: it includes gathering materials, crafting a weapon, finding the wild boar, fighting the battle, cooking the dish. Everything related to gameplay has been marked with underlining.

Evidently, gameplay is a thing that links two resources, and it shares a certain common character. And this common character is: logic. Meanwhile, when multiple logics are linked together, there forms: the logic chain.

## **Logic and Logic Chains**

It has been shown that a logic chain represents one gameplay process within a game. Next, the function of the logic chain will be analyzed, starting from the simplest one-step logic chain.

### **The Length of a Logic Chain**

The simplest logic in the world is driving screws on an assembly line — repeating one action an infinite number of times with no new variables arising. This is the one-step logic chain. Obviously, if a game has one and only one thing to do (for example, endlessly moving the parts on the left into the box on the right), then this game most likely intends to reflect something absurdist, or to satirize the repetitive toil of workers under capitalism.

And in contrast, look back once more at Awei's survival game. In this example, for a pot of meat stew, Awei carried out a logic chain six steps long — and this made Awei feel happy and comfortable, self-fulfilled.

Why is this so? By certain popular sayings, shouldn't games be as simple as possible? Perhaps, as a certain great man said, work is not repetitive toil, but making good use of the resources around you — whether finite or infinite — to solve the problems you face. An oversimplified game can only make people feel as if they have entered the factory to drive screws (many scripts were born precisely to combat this kind of dull, oversimplified game mechanic).

The psychological satisfaction that long logic chains bring players can be roughly explained as follows: when a player completes one piece of work, he will inevitably begin conceiving the next step, and the step after that — this brings the player a sense of anticipation; and when the player truly completes one long logic chain, the player gains a satisfaction of self-actualization.

Speaking of anticipation, planning, and realization, one cannot avoid mentioning the currently popular doctrines of goal management and stage division. Many games have the concept of "tech levels" — what players jokingly call "Tier 1, Tier 2." From a certain angle, this is precisely a method that helps players conduct goal management, dividing the entire game's content into different stages.

Therefore, if you wish to construct a charming game with depth of playability, you should extend the game's logic chains as far as possible to increase players' anticipation and satisfaction. But note as well: an overly long logic chain may exceed the ceiling of what players can bear, and should be simplified as appropriate.

But... is one long logic chain enough? Sometimes the logic chain cannot be extended any further (for example Awei and the meat stew — it is hard to imagine any room for extension in that process). At this point one has no choice but to consider adding multiple logic chains to increase the game's interest. So, what will be expounded next is: systems formed of multiple logic chains.

### **Systems Formed of Logic Chains**

The importance of systems need not be said. In every mainstream game theory, we can see that games are divided into different systems, such as the combat system, the cooking system, the gathering system... So this theory likewise adopts the concept of the system. A system is now defined as follows:

A series of logic chains whose prerequisite resources are the same or different, but whose finally produced resources share at least partly the same utility, may be called a system.

This is not an entirely accurate definition; counterexamples can be raised with ease, such as: crafting an axe by collecting materials clearly belongs to the crafting system, while buying an axe from a shop clearly belongs to the trading system.
